## Background

This work begins with a very ordinary question: beyond being torn, pasted, and used for fixing, can tape also become a sound material? Tape stretches, sticks, rubs, rebounds, and breaks. When it is slowly pulled out, guided around a bearing, pressed against another surface, or forced to move under changing tension, it produces small but layered sounds.

I combined the reel structure of a cassette player, the gesture of using a tape dispenser, and material sound experiments. The action of "opening a roll of tape" is broken down into a mechanical process that can be observed, controlled, and repeated. The work invites viewers to notice that sound does not only come from instruments or playback devices; it is also hidden in contact, resistance, and material movement.

## Structural Translation

The core of the installation is a double-reel structure. Tape is held between rollers on both sides, and continuous movement is produced through turning and pulling, similar to how magnetic tape is read inside a machine. The difference is that what is "read" here is not pre-recorded information, but the friction, adhesion, and mechanical sound generated by the tape itself.

In the structural design, I focused on three relationships: the contact area between tape and roller, tension changes during movement, and the stability of the device during manual operation. To make sound experiments repeatable and comparable, the installation uses screws, bearings, transparent sheets, and supports to form a clear framework that retains a mechanical quality while making the tape path visible.

## Sound Experiments

Before final fabrication, I tested different materials and triggering methods: tapes with different widths and adhesion, plastic sheets, metal parts, paper surfaces, manual pulling, reel rotation, and surface friction. Each combination changes the grain and rhythm of the sound. Peeling tape from a surface produces a continuous tearing texture, while moving it around a roller creates steadier mechanical vibration.

These experiments helped me understand sound less as a fixed result and more as a process. The same roll of tape can present completely different textures under different speeds, angles, and forces. The installation therefore does not play a fixed sound; it creates conditions in which the material can be operated and perceived.

## Making Process

The making process focused on structural stability and the perceptibility of sound. Early stages used drawings to define reel, support, and connector positions. Midway through, materials were purchased, drilled, cut, and test-assembled. In the final stage, the tape path was repeatedly adjusted so that the tape could move smoothly through the rollers while producing clear sound.

The hardest part was balancing smooth operation with resistance. If the structure was too smooth, the sound became weak; if resistance was too high, the tape could jam or tear. The final device keeps a degree of manual control, allowing speed, angle, and force to directly shape the sound.
